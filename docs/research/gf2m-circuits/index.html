Fewer gates for the arithmetic inside AES

AES, the cipher that protects most web traffic and encrypted disks, does its mixing in a strange 256-number world. Swapnil Jain, searching with AI help and checking every result by machine, cut the wiring for its multiplication from 117 gates to 112 and built its one nonlinear step from 106 gates. Aska added multipliers for four more number systems.

Machine-verified · Lean-proved Not peer reviewed No optimality claimed repo at 9daf883 · checked 13 Oct 2026

Swapnil Jain’s repository and two preprints. Aska Digital contributed one pull request, merged as #1 on 11 October 2026: verified multiplier circuits for four more number systems, built with the papers’ own method. GF(29) in 136 gates against a 142-gate baseline, GF(210) in 172 (equal to its baseline), GF(211) in 199 against 206, and the GHASH field GF(2128) in 11,972 (equal). They are not records, sit outside the author’s hash commitment, and have no Lean proofs.

00//Gates

First principles

A computer multiplies with switches.

Underneath every program are gates: tiny switches that take two bits and give back one. An AND gate says “both”. An XOR gate says “exactly one”. Wire enough of them together and you can compute anything. The question here is how few you need.

The arithmetic in question is strange but simple. Write a number as 8 bits. To add two numbers, XOR them bit by bit, with no carries. To multiply, do grade-school long multiplication, but add the columns with XOR instead of ordinary addition. The answer spills past 8 bits, so fold the overflow back in by a fixed rule: wherever x8 appears, write x4 + x3 + x + 1 instead.

Definition

GF(28), a finite field: the 256 numbers you can write in 8 bits, with that addition and that multiplication. Every rule of ordinary arithmetic still holds, and every number except 0 has an inverse you can divide by.

AES, the cipher that protects most web traffic and disk encryption, does all of its mixing in this field. Its one nonlinear step, the S-box, takes each byte’s inverse in the field and then scrambles the bits.

Why count gates? On a chip, each gate costs area and power. In “bitsliced” software, each gate is one processor instruction, applied to many blocks at once. Building the arithmetic from gates also avoids lookup tables, which can leak secrets through how long memory takes to answer.

A circuit’s depth is the longest chain of gates a signal passes through, one after another. It stands in for how long the answer takes.

Why is it hard? The number of possible wirings explodes, and even a computer cannot search them all: an exact search for a 16-input circuit of more than 100 gates is out of reach. So records sit still for years. The 117-gate multiplier for the AES field dated from July 2010. Nobody can currently prove that any of these circuits is the smallest possible, and the papers claim no such thing.

01//A 16-number world

Live circuit

Long multiplication, without the carries.

Shrink the field to 4 bits and the whole circuit fits on a screen. The 16 numbers multiply by the same recipe, with the fold rule x4 = x + 1. Pick two numbers and watch 30 gates compute their product.

The schoolbook circuit multiplies every bit of a by every bit of b: 16 ANDs. Jain’s circuit first forms a few sums of input bits (8 XORs), multiplies those sums in pairs (only 10 ANDs), then combines the 10 products into the 4 output bits, doing the fold at the same time (12 XORs). Spending a little on preparation buys fewer multiplications.

That is Karatsuba’s 1962 trick, generalised: to multiply two 2-part numbers you need only 3 products instead of 4, if you pay a few extra additions first. For four bits the trick saves one gate overall. For the AES field it saves more, as the next section shows.

02//The size–speed staircase

Lean-proved

Smaller or faster: pick one.

There is no single best circuit. At the 2010 record’s speed, multiplication in the AES field drops from 117 gates to 112. Allow twice the depth, and it drops to 107.

Every multiplier of this kind can be drawn in three layers: prepare (XORs that form sums of input bits), multiply (ANDs that multiply pairs of those sums) and clean up (XORs that combine the products into the 8 output bits, folding as they go).

Jain’s search samples many recursive mixes of schoolbook splits and Karatsuba splits, solves exactly for the best clean-up layer of each, and scores every candidate by its total gate count. The earlier approach of Find and Peralta minimised the number of ANDs first.

Compare the layers of the 2010 record and the new 112-gate circuit at the same depth: preparation grows from 8 XORs to 20, but the ANDs fall from 48 to 36 and the clean-up from 61 XORs to 56. Different products, cheaper overall.

Not “the smallest ever”

The Circuit Minimization Team also lists a 106-gate, depth-6 multiplier from 2017 that works in an undisclosed “tower” layout of the field. Using it with AES’s standard layout needs conversions of unknown cost, so the preprint does not compare against it, and neither do we.

03//AES’s secret ingredient

Lean-provedAND / XOR / XNOR model only

The one step of AES that is not just XOR, in 106 gates.

Most of AES is XORs and fixed shuffles. The S-box replaces each byte by its inverse in the 256-number world, then scrambles the bits. Software usually looks it up in a 256-entry table; built from gates, it needs no table, so there is no secret-dependent memory access for a timing attack to watch. Run the actual circuit here.

The 106-gate circuit came from a sweep over 244,800 ways of writing the field, followed by random walks that keep the gate count while reshaping the circuit. Walks within AES’s usual way of writing the field stalled at 110. The search code is not released, so these circuits can be checked by anyone but not regenerated.

The comparison is honest about its limits. The 106 is smaller than NIST’s 110 but deeper, 24 against 22. NIST’s own list asks for 105 gates or fewer, so the 106 is one gate short of that wish. And the comparison holds only for circuits made of AND, XOR and XNOR gates.

04//Aska’s part

Contributed · merged PR #1Verified · not records · no Lean

Four more number systems, checked, and not records.

Our agentic team applied the papers’ method to fields of 9, 10, 11 and 128 bits that had no dedicated published circuit, and Swapnil Jain merged the result the same day. You can re-check three of them on every input, here.

The method was Jain’s, applied to new fields. Take the published product circuit of Find and Peralta for m-bit polynomials, keep its products, merge the fold into the clean-up layer, and re-optimise both XOR layers with the repository’s own tool. Then sweep the depth, and try small exact searches (“window SAT”) for local improvements; thousands of windows were tried and none improved.

What it gained. GF(29) came in 6 gates below its baseline and GF(211) 7 below; GF(210) and the GHASH field of AES-GCM only matched theirs, and say “no gain claimed”. The baseline is the published product circuit plus a textbook fold, not a published record: for these fields no dedicated circuit was found. The AND counts (54, 52, 78) are exactly Find and Peralta’s, so every gate saved is an XOR.

Stated plainly

Not records. Our pull request first called them “first published (no record)”; the maintainer reworded that to “no dedicated published circuit found”, which is the accurate claim.

Weaker checks than Jain’s own circuits. They are verified exhaustively (GHASH symbolically, since 2256 input pairs cannot be listed), but they have no Lean proofs and sit outside the author’s hash commitment.

Not a drop-in GHASH. The GF(2128) circuit uses plain polynomial bit order; GCM’s reflected bit order is not addressed.

Open, not merged: a second pull request (#2, opened 13 Oct 2026) adds more fields and is not yet reviewed by the maintainer, so it is not counted here.

05//How anyone can check

Re-run by Foundation0 · 13 Oct 2026

The search was AI-assisted. The trust comes from somewhere else.

Both preprints say: “Computational search and drafting were assisted by AI tools; all released circuits were machine-verified.” No tool is named. What makes the circuits believable is that anyone can check them in seconds.

  1. 1

    Every input

    Each circuit is run on every possible input, 65,536 pairs for an AES-field multiplier and 256 bytes for an S-box, against an independent reference. This page does it again in your browser.

  2. 2

    Every formula

    Each output bit’s exact algebraic formula must equal the one multiplication defines. This is how the 128-bit circuits are checked, where listing inputs is impossible.

  3. 3

    Broken on purpose

    Deliberately damaged circuits and a wrong field must be rejected. That shows the checker can say no; it is not extra evidence for the circuits.

  4. 4

    Machine proof

    Lean 4’s kernel proves 30 of the circuits correct. The theorem checks a generated gate list; for the S-box it proves equality with a written-out table, and that the table is AES’s rests on the Python verifier. Gate counts and depths are not Lean-checked.

  5. 5

    Committed in advance

    SHA-256 fingerprints of the circuit files were posted in two public gists on 11 Oct 2026, before the preprints. The author calls this a weak timestamp, since a gist can be edited.

What Foundation0 re-ran

On a scratch copy of the repository: the multiplier verifier on every committed circuit and on Aska’s (all pass, with gate counts exactly as published); the S-box verifier on all 11 circuits (output identical to the saved results); the commitment fingerprints (both match the live gists); and five of the Lean proofs, including the 106-gate S-box, with the official Lean 4.34.1 release. The generator rebuilds the Lean file for the 112-gate circuit byte for byte from the circuit file, so the proof is about that exact circuit.

What remains open

Both papers are single-author preprints from October 2026, posted on ResearchGate and not peer reviewed. “Smallest known” is only as good as the author’s literature check, which the author calls “not a systematic survey”. The author knows of no non-trivial lower bound on total gates, so nobody can say how close any of these circuits is to the smallest possible.

The field is busy: NIST’s list was updated on 6 October 2026 and records S-box circuits contributed by Milad Nasr of Anthropic on 24 September 2026.
