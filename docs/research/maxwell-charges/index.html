Physics · a physics0 project · first study

How many points of balance?

Put a few positive electric charges in space. Between them are points where a test charge would feel no force at all. In 1873 James Clerk Maxwell wrote that n charges make at most (n − 1)2 such points. In July 2026 that was shown false for five charges. We counted exactly what four, five and six charges can do, and a computer checks every count.

00//Balance

First principles

Where the pushes cancel.

Two positive charges push each other apart. Put a third, small test charge somewhere near them: it is pushed by both. At one point on the line between them the two pushes are equal and opposite, and the test charge feels nothing. That point is an equilibrium, a point of balance.

With more charges there are more such points, scattered between them. Each is a place where the electric field, the push a test charge would feel, is exactly zero.

Definition

The potential V(x) = q1/|x − a1| + … + qn/|x − an| measures how hard it is to bring a test charge to the point x. The field points downhill on it. An equilibrium is a point where V is flat in every direction: a critical point.

No equilibrium is stable. That is Earnshaw’s theorem (1842): a test charge always has some direction in which it slides away. So the points come in two kinds:

  • a 1-saddle holds the test charge in a whole plane but lets it escape along one line;
  • a 2-saddle holds it along one line but lets it escape across a whole plane, like the point between two charges.

A theorem from topology, Morse theory, fixes the balance between the two kinds. For n positive charges, when no equilibrium is degenerate (flat to higher order), there are always exactly n − 1 more 2-saddles than 1-saddles. So the total is

equilibria = (n − 1) + 2 × (number of 1-saddles).

Counting equilibria is the same as counting 1-saddles. Three equal charges on a triangle have one 1-saddle, at the centre, and three 2-saddles near the edges: 4 in all.

In his 1873 Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism (§113), Maxwell asserted that n charges have at most (n − 1)2 equilibria. His argument had a gap. In 2007 Andrei Gabrielov, Dmitry Novikov and Boris Shapiro made it a precise conjecture, and for nearly 20 years nobody could prove or refute it.

Why it matters

Counting critical points of physical potentials is a basic question of mathematical physics: it tells you how complicated the field of a few charges, masses or vortices can be. This one has been open since Maxwell, and in 2026 AI-assisted work started moving it.

01//Scoreboard

As of 18 Oct 2026

Maxwell’s bound now fails for five, six and seven charges.

For each number of charges: Maxwell’s bound, and the most equilibria anyone has shown. Three and four charges are still open; for eight and more no configuration above the bound is known.

The counterexample

Philip Arathoon, Gavin Ball and Matthew Kvalheim posted “The Maxwell Conjecture is False” on 29 July 2026. They say the construction was suggested by OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 Sol, and that they checked every detail and wrote the argument themselves. Put three unit charges on an equilateral triangle, then two tiny charges on its axis, just above and below the centre, with their strength tuned to their distance. The triangle’s centre, a 1-saddle, splits into 21 points of balance, while the three edge points survive: 24, against Maxwell’s 16. On the Ledger.

What their paper leaves open, and what we added

Their proof shows at least 24 for small enough tuning, then nudges the charges by an unspecified amount to rule out degenerate points elsewhere. We give exact rational charges and positions and prove there are exactly 24, all non-degenerate, with nothing else anywhere. Two related facts: four charges can reach Maxwell’s 9, and six charges can have 29.

02//Certified

Interval arithmetic · Krawczyk test

How a computer can prove a count.

A computer cannot try every point in space, and ordinary arithmetic on it rounds. A certificate gets around both. Every number is replaced by a range that is guaranteed to contain the true value, and space is cut into boxes until each box is settled one way or the other.

Definition

Interval arithmetic: compute with ranges instead of numbers, rounding every lower end down and every upper end up. If the range for the field at a box does not include zero, there is no equilibrium anywhere in that box. That is a proof, not an estimate.

The program starts from a box around all the charges. With positive charges, every equilibrium lies inside the region they span, so nothing outside needs checking. It then halves boxes again and again. A box is settled when one of three things happens:

  1. the range for the field excludes zero, so the box is empty;
  2. one charge is so close that its push outweighs all the others combined, so again the box is empty;
  3. the box passes the Krawczyk test, a version of Newton’s method run on ranges. It proves that the box holds exactly one equilibrium, and that the equilibrium is not degenerate.

When every box is settled, the count of Krawczyk boxes is the count of equilibria, with no rounding error anywhere. The kind of each point, 1-saddle or 2-saddle, is proved the same way.

We wrote the check twice. The main program uses ordinary computer arithmetic with every result rounded outward. The second one shares no code with it: it uses Arb ball arithmetic and exact fractions, reads only the charges and the boxes from each certificate, and redoes the whole proof. Both agree on all four counts.

Break it

A checker that always says yes proves nothing. So the tests hand the second program four damaged certificates: one point removed, one point listed twice, one point given the wrong kind, and one charge changed. It rejects all four. 03//Six charges

A tiny charge on a balance point

One tiny charge turns one point of balance into six.

The four- and six-charge results come from the same simple move: drop a very small charge exactly onto a 1-saddle that looks the same from every side around an axis.

Near a 1-saddle the field is held in a plane and escapes along a line. A tiny charge placed there pushes outward in every direction. In the plane, its outward push is matched by the inward hold on a small ring around it, so a ring of balance appears. If the 1-saddle is perfectly symmetric about its axis, the ring is a whole circle of balance points. The next, three-fold term of the field breaks the circle into six points, alternating 1-saddles and 2-saddles. One point of balance becomes six, so the count goes up by 5.

The triangle’s centre is such a point. That gives four charges with 4 − 1 + 6 = 9 equilibria, exactly Maxwell’s bound. The centre of the five-charge counterexample is such a point too. That gives six charges with 24 − 1 + 6 = 29.

If the 1-saddle is not symmetric, the same move gives four points instead of six: a gain of 3. Our note proves both cases in outline. The certificates do not depend on that outline; they check the final configurations directly.

How new

Both moves are simple, and specialists may well know them. We found no earlier configuration with nine equilibria for four charges, or more than 25 for six. The 2026 paper by Edelsbrunner, Fillmore and Oliveira states that no configuration reaching (n − 1)2 was known for n > 3. If you know earlier work, please tell us.

04//Still open

Evidence, not proof

Three charges, four charges, and many.

Maxwell may still be right for three and four charges, and may be right again for large numbers of charges. Here is what we looked at, and what we found.

Three charges

The bound now stands between 4 and 6. For three charges there is a neat reformulation. Each point inside the triangle is a point of balance for exactly one choice of charge ratios, which follows from Lami’s theorem. Maxwell’s bound then says that this map from points to charge ratios never reaches the same ratios from two different local minima. We checked that over 1,289 triangle shapes. In the 799 where the region of minima shows up at our grid’s resolution, no ratio was reached twice; three doubled pixels at cusps were artifacts that a direct solve ruled out. The other shapes, mostly very flat, showed no minima at that resolution, so for them the evidence is weak.

Four charges

No four-charge configuration with more than 9 turned up in several searches, together tens of thousands of configurations. They included a fine scan of the symmetric family, random sampling across many scales, hill-climbing, and a least-squares search for four 1-saddles sharing one set of charges, which never converged. A local model explains the ceiling: one small charge in a smooth field can create at most 4 nearby points, or 6 with symmetry, so “three charges plus one” stops at 9.

Many charges

Every known construction grows in step with the number of charges, about 20 more equilibria for every two charges added. Maxwell’s bound grows with the square. So from eight charges up, no known configuration beats it. Whether it fails for large n needs a new idea. We also found that the counterexample’s central structure is already the best its design allows: its 21 central points are all the solutions its local equations can have.
