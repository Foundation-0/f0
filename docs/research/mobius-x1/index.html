Beating a 1937 recipe, eventually

An AI-assisted proof claims that a basic operation on bits, the one that turns a logic function’s table of answers into its formula, can be done with fewer XOR gates than Frank Yates’ 89-year-old method. The catch: the win appears only at sizes no computer will ever see.

Not peer reviewed · AI-refereed only Main theorem not in Lean Asymptotic only repo at 00ffede · checked 14 Oct 2026

Swapnil Jain’s repository and claim. Aska Digital contributed one piece: our agentic team, run by founding member Ahraz Arifuddin (GitHub ahrazzle), wrote and ran the full-network check of the “E1” gadget that Theorem 2 depends on. It ran every invocation at sizes h = 8, 9 and 10 and a symmetry-reduced run at h = 11, found zero failures, and showed that two deliberately broken versions fail as they should. Swapnil Jain merged it as pull request #1 on 11 October 2026.

00//The problem

First principles

One bit for every combination.

Take k pizza toppings. There are 2k ways to choose some of them. Give each choice one bit, 0 or 1. Now ask, for every choice: do the bits on it and on all of its smaller choices add up to an odd number or an even one?

With three toppings a, b and c there are N = 8 choices: none, a, b, ab, c, ac, bc, abc. The answer for “ab” looks at the bits of ab, a, b and none, adds them, and keeps only odd or even. The list of all N answers is called the Möbius transform over GF(2), also the zeta, subset-parity or Sierpinski transform.

Two words

GF(2): arithmetic with only 0 and 1, where 1 + 1 = 0. Adding is then the XOR gate: two bits in, and out comes 1 if exactly one of them is 1. A circuit’s size is how many XOR gates it uses.

Why care? This transform turns a logic function’s table of answers into its formula (its “algebraic normal form”), which is how cryptanalysts read a cipher’s building blocks. It sits inside Reed–Muller error-correcting codes and inside fast algorithms that sum over subsets. And it is one of the simplest places to ask a deep question: can letting things cancel ever save work?

Draw a grid with one row per output and one column per input, and put a dot where the column’s toppings are all inside the row’s. You get Pascal’s triangle with its odd entries lit: the picture at the top of this page. That is Lucas’s theorem of 1878 in action.

The obvious method adds up every sub-choice separately. That costs 3k − 2k XORs. In 1937 the statistician Frank Yates found the recipe everyone still uses: handle one topping at a time. For each choice that has topping t, add in the value of its twin without t. That is k passes of N/2 XORs each, ½ N log2 N in all, drawn below as a “butterfly”.

Below: pick k, change the input bits, and step through Yates’ passes. The outputs always match the grid.

01//The open question

89 years of Yates

Without cancellation, nobody can do better. With it, nobody knew.

A circuit “cancels” when the same input reaches a gate along two paths, so it adds in twice and disappears, because x  x = 0. Yates’ butterfly never does this. Is that a weakness, or the best there is?

In 2012 and 2013 Joan Boyar and Magnus Gausdal Find proved that if a circuit is not allowed to cancel, ½ N log2 N gates is exactly the minimum for this transform: Yates cannot be beaten on those terms. They then asked to what extent cancellation can help. For the smallest sizes they checked by computer that it does not help at all.

In 2024 Igor Sergeev still listed “can it be done in fewer than n·2n−1 gates?” as an open problem. The best proven lower bound is only about 3N, so the true answer could sit anywhere between 3N and Yates’ ½ N log2 N.

  1. 1878

    Édouard Lucas: a binomial coefficient is odd exactly when its binary digits nest. Both of the claim’s parity tricks use it.

  2. 1937

    Frank Yates’ recipe: k passes of N/2 XORs.

  3. 2012–13

    Boyar and Find: optimal without cancellation; does cancellation help? (arXiv 1207.5321, 1305.3041)

  4. 2024

    Sergeev lists the question as open; lower bound (3 − o(1))·N (slides).

  5. 6 Oct 2026

    OpenAI publishes a manuscript (dated 25 Sep), produced by an internal OpenAI model, that beats n log n for the exact complex Fourier transform (openai/math problem 130).

  6. 11 Oct 2026

    Swapnil Jain publishes mobius-x1, a claimed yes for bits. Aska’s full-network check is merged the same day.

02//Garbage that erases itself

The clever part

Add it twice and it is gone.

The new circuit borrows helper wires that already hold leftover values, lets that junk flow into the answer, then sends the same junk in a second time so that it cancels. What remains is exactly the signal, and every helper wire ends where it started.

The figure runs the claim’s eight-row “gadget” on single bits. Four wires: x is the value to deliver, y the place to deliver it, and A and c are helpers holding unknown junk a0 and c0. Each row adds one wire into another, if its switch is on.

Rows 0 and 1 pour junk into y. Rows 2 and 3 load x into the helpers. Rows 4 and 5 pour the helpers into y again: the junk arrives a second time and vanishes, and x arrives along each route that is switched on. Rows 6 and 7 take x back out of the helpers.

So the whole trick rests on one count. If x reaches y along an odd number of routes, y gains x. If the count is even, x cancels too and nothing is delivered. Switch both routes on to watch the gadget fail.

From bits to the real thing

In the real network each route is a bundle of shared “centre” wires, and the switches R, G, J and V are matrices. The condition becomes RG + JV = I mod 2: every label must reach itself an odd number of times and every other label an even number of times. Three of these gadgets in a row make the old XOR-swap trick: (X, Y) → (X, X+Y) → (Y, X+Y) → (Y, X).

Choosing the centre: Lucas again

In the strongest version (Theorem 2, the “E1” gadget) every label is a set of 7 points out of h, and the shared centre wires are all the 3-point sets. Two labels S and T that overlap in n points share every 3-point set inside the overlap: C(n, 3) wires. The gadget needs that count to be odd when S = T, so a label’s own data survives, and even for unrelated labels, so their data cancels.

C(n, 3) is odd only at n = 3 and n = 7. That is Lucas’s rule from the triangle above: 3 is 011 in binary, and only 3 (011) and 7 (111) contain it. Overlaps that are a multiple of 3 are what the proof calls neighbours; there an odd count is allowed but must be patched by a side wire (the J route). With this centre only overlaps of exactly 3 need one, and fewer side wires mean a better exponent.

Swap in 2-point sets as the centre and an overlap of 2 gives C(2, 2) = 1, odd, between unrelated labels: junk leaks into the answer. Aska’s check ran that broken version on purpose, and it failed in 277,200 places.

03//The claim

Claimed · AI-refereed only

A machine built for complex numbers, rebuilt for bits.

The claim is that the transform needs only about N·(log N)θ′ XOR gates with θ′ a hair below 1, which is eventually smaller than Yates’ ½ N log2 N. Here is how it is built, in five steps.

  1. 1

    Borrow an AI-found machine

    A preprint dated 25 September 2026 and made public on 6 October in OpenAI’s openai/math collection (problem 130), “produced by an internal OpenAI model”, beats n log n for the exact complex Fourier transform. Its recursive network saves a tiny fraction of steps at every level; recursion compounds that, and the exponent drops below 1.

  2. 2

    Flip the arithmetic

    Complex numbers do not exist among bits, so Jain swaps the roles: labels are counted mod 3, and data live in F4, a four-element number system with a cube root of one, ω. The 3×3 building block becomes C′ = ωI + ω2J3.

  3. 3

    Make the junk cancel by parity

    The centre is chosen with Lucas’s theorem so that every unwanted contribution arrives an even number of times: the gadget in section 02.

  4. 4

    Remove a correction step

    A cheap relabelling applied on both the input side and the output side replaces one correction step per bank entry, doubling the saving. A one-sided version fails: it works for 0 of the 42 test lines checked.

  5. 5

    Come back to bits

    An explicit 12-slot “word” writes two levels of the transform as a product of copies of the building block over F16, and every F16 gate costs at most 28 XORs. So a fast C′ gives a fast Möbius transform. Lean checks that the word’s 27 factors, 12 of them copies of the building block, multiply out to exactly two levels of the transform.

Overclaims, caught

The AI-assisted drafts overclaimed twice, and a later check caught both: an exponent that leaned on an unproved recurrence, and an “ESTABLISHED” verdict that was softened to “claimed” four minutes after the first public commit. An earlier AI-drafted argument that a correction step is unavoidable was wrong and was retracted. The exponent and the retraction are logged in CORRECTIONS.md; the verdict change is in the commit history.

04//How far away is “eventually”?

The honest scale

Right, perhaps, and still slower on every computer that will ever exist.

“Asymptotically fewer” means fewer once the input is large enough. The new circuit carries a bigger constant cost, and its advantage over Yates grows like k1−θ′, which with 1 − θ′ = 0.0000000000437 barely grows at all.

Write the new circuit’s cost as c0 times Yates’, divided by k1−θ′. Here k is the number of toppings and c0 the ratio of the two methods’ constant factors; the repository works out c0 = 84 for its explicit word and also reports 2 and 106 as brackets. The new circuit wins once k1−θ′ passes c0. Slide to find where.

  • The step count is exact. Counting every step and weighting by symmetry gives the proof’s closed formula at every size: 5,190,901,996,500 steps at h = 11, on both sides.
  • Merged and credited. Swapnil Jain merged it the same day: “Thank you, this closes the full-network gap for Theorem 2 nicely, with both controls refuted.” The README now credits ahrazzle. 1 of the repository’s 6 commits, +500 lines (8% of its current lines).

What the check does not show

Small sizes only. The runs reach h = 11; the theorem uses h = 23. The large-h counts in the repository are re-derived from a formula, not independently checked.

Side wires are exercised only at h = 11. At h ≤ 10 the E1 gadget has no side wires at all, so the all-invocation runs never touch them; at h = 11 they are checked on representative invocations.

No speed-up is witnessed. At every size checked the gadget costs more than it saves (at h = 11 the per-level saving is −1,114,047,000 steps; it turns positive only from h = 20). The check confirms that the network is correct and that the step-count formula is exact.

A careful port, not an independent implementation. About two thirds of the checker’s distinct lines mirror Swapnil Jain’s earlier x1_e2e_rep.c; the 7-set combinatorics and side-wire logic are new.

Two stale lines upstream. The README’s headline table still says “no full 7-set network has been run”, and its credit line mentions representative runs at h = 11 and 12, though the only h = 12 log is the broken control. We report h = 8–11.
