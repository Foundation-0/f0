Algorithms · a founding member’s race, with the crowd

Shaving the logarithm

For fifty years the best guess was that multiplying two huge numbers needs about n log n steps, and no fewer. A machine-written paper claimed a sliver below that. In one week a public race of people and their AI agents made the sliver about 1052 times wider. It is still a sliver, and all of it rests on that unreviewed paper.

00//The problem

First principles

Every digit meets every digit.

Before asking how fast multiplication can go, count the work in the method everyone learns at school.

Write two numbers, each n digits long, one above the other. The school method multiplies every digit of one by every digit of the other, then adds up the rows. That is n × n small multiplications: a 4-digit problem takes 16, a 1,000-digit problem a million. Double the length and the work goes up four times. This growth is written n², “n squared”.

Definition

Step: one elementary operation, like multiplying two single digits. Methods are compared by how their number of steps grows as n grows, ignoring fixed multipliers (the “constants”), because for big enough numbers the growth decides who wins.

Computers work in binary, so for them n counts bits (binary digits) instead of decimal digits, but the counting is the same. And the numbers can be enormous: encryption multiplies numbers thousands of bits long, and record computations of π multiply numbers with billions of digits.

In 1960 the mathematician Andrey Kolmogorov conjectured that nothing could beat n². Within weeks, Anatoly Karatsuba, a student at Kolmogorov’s seminar, did. Split each number into a front half and a back half. Of the four half-size products the school method would need, compute only three: the two “matching” ones, and one product of sums that delivers the other two added together. Repeat the trick inside every half, and only 3 of every 4 products survive at every level. The work grows like n1.585 instead of n2. The figure shows it.

01//The floor

1962 → 2026

From n² down to n log n, and a guess about the floor.

Karatsuba opened a sixty-year chase. Its last big step treats numbers as waves.

The digits of a number are like a sound sampled over time. Multiplying two numbers is, apart from carrying, a calculation called a convolution: slide one row of digits past the other and add up the overlapping products. The figure does exactly that, one position at a time. The fast Fourier transform (FFT), the method that splits a sound into its notes, turns a convolution into note-by-note multiplication, which is cheap: in effect, it does all the sliding at once. Converting into notes and back costs about n log n steps.

Definition

log n (the base-2 logarithm) is roughly how many times you can halve n before reaching 1: about 20 for a million, about 30 for a billion. It grows so slowly that n log n is only a few dozen times the cost of reading the numbers once.

Arnold Schönhage and Volker Strassen used the FFT in 1971 to reach n log n log log n, and guessed that n log n is the floor: that no method can do better. In 2019 David Harvey and Joris van der Hoeven finally reached n log n; the proof appeared in the Annals of Mathematics in 2021. Most people took that as the end of the road.

These bounds are proved on a multitape Turing machine: a deliberately simple model of a computer that reads and writes a few long tapes one cell at a time, so even moving data around costs steps. It is the standard yardstick because it cannot hide work inside clever hardware.

On 6 October 2026 OpenAI published a manuscript, dated 23 September, titled “Integer multiplication below n log n”. Its only listed author is “OpenAI”, and the repository that holds it says its manuscripts were “produced by an internal OpenAI model”. It is result family #109 of 722 machine-written manuscripts. It claims multiplication in

T(n) = n · (log n)1−κwith κ = 2−182

If κ were 0, this would be n log n. Any κ above 0, however small, means n log n is not the floor on this machine. And 2−182 is small: written as a decimal it starts with 54 zeros.

Read with care

The preprint is not peer reviewed, and it is not among the results OpenAI has formalized in Lean, a proof checker. Its own repository warns that “some of the unformalized results could have issues.” Everything below is conditional on it being right.

02//How much is κ worth?

Computed in your browser

A saving that grows forever, absurdly slowly.

The race’s number is not a speed-up anyone will notice. Its value is what it would prove.

Ignoring constants, the race’s method beats n log n by a factor of (log n)κ. For million-bit numbers and today’s κ, that is half a percent. For numbers 264 bits long, about 2.3 million terabytes each, it is still under one percent. To make multiplication even twice as fast as n log n, the numbers would need about 210180 bits; the observable universe holds about 1080 atoms.

The constants ignored here are, in the preprint’s own words, “extremely large”. Nobody’s computer multiplies faster because of this race, and nobody involved claims it does. What a proven κ > 0 would show is a fact about the problem itself: on this machine, n log n is not the floor. A bigger κ is a sturdier, more checkable version of that fact.

03//The race

Conditional · maintainer-reviewed

From 2−182 to 2−9 in one week.

On 7 October Doug Colkitt opened a public repository to widen the sliver, and invited anyone to “donate inference”: point an AI agent at a task and send back a result a computer can check exactly.

Within 22 hours, working with OpenAI Codex, Doug Colkitt had pushed κ from 2−182 past 2−34 by tightening the preprint’s own construction. Then the crowd arrived: more than 1,300 pull requests (proposed changes, which the maintainer can review and merge) in a week, many written with AI agents and disclosed as such.

Every improvement had to arrive with exact-arithmetic certificates, fractions computed with no rounding, and scripts anyone can re-run. That rule is what made a crowd usable: nobody has to trust the sender, because anyone can run their checker. The maintainer reviews, re-runs and selects; each selection becomes the new record.

Two true pictures. On the halvings scale the first 48 hours look like everything: restructuring the preprint’s construction crossed most of the distance, from 2−182 to 2−30. On the plain scale those leaps vanish into the floor, and the last days look like the whole story: 0.00072 to 0.00167 as many people and agents fought over fractions of a percent.

What the record is now. The live value at the top of this page comes straight from the race’s certificates/selected-result.json. If it is newer than the last step drawn here, the figure adds it as a “now” step. The record changes several times a day, so every number on this page carries a date.

04//Where the saving comes from

Exact data from PRs #450, #464, #469

A budget that must stay under one.

Inside the proof, κ comes out of a bill: hand down slightly less work than you take in, and the leftover compounds into a saving.

The algorithm is recursive: it splits a job into many smaller jobs, splits those again, and so on. Think of one job as a budget of 1. One building block in the construction has width m = 78 and W = 1,200 roles, and hands out child jobs of different sizes r: 27,060 of size 1, fewer of each larger size, and 184 of size 56.

Charged at their plain size, the children cost 93,192/93,600 = 0.99564 of the budget. There is a margin of 0.44%, and that margin is the whole game.

To bank a saving a, each child of size r is billed as if it were a little bigger, by a factor (m/r)a. Small children are billed the most, because m/r is largest for them. Raise a and the bill climbs. The largest a that keeps the bill under 1 is this block’s saving, and the outer algorithm turns it into κ = a/(1 + a). The current construction has two kinds of block, called bit and complex, and the weaker of the two sets κ.

That makes the lesson of the figure plain: moving work out of the smallest child jobs and into slightly bigger ones, with the total unchanged, lets a climb. Much of the race is exactly this kind of tidying, done by people and agents in parallel and checked to the last digit.

Where these numbers come from

The bill is bill(a) = Σr nr (r/m)1−a / W, plus a tiny insurance charge for rare bad cases, as written in the race’s autoresearch.md and research/r480-sharp-charge535. The child counts nr are the published ones for PR #450’s block and Aska’s PR #469. Your browser finds each break-even a by halving an interval 80 times and reproduces every published digit of κ; we checked the same three roots separately with 60-digit arithmetic.

04.1//Our part, at its true size

Claimed · never reviewedClosed by Aska, 13 Oct

On 11 October, working with Claude Fable 5.1, Aska Digital submitted two changes to the block from PR #450, by the contributor DreamingOfClouds.

PR #464 replaced an over-cautious insurance charge for rare bad cases with the smaller figure the source theorem actually proves. The idea was not ours: Srikrishnabalaji found it first, in PR #461, and applied it to a different block; upstream later credited #464 for carrying it over. κ rose by about 3 × 10−12.

PR #469 kept every addition in the block and re-timed 113 data moves into coarser frames, which shifted work out of the smallest child jobs: 649 fewer jobs of size 1, 99 fewer of size 7, 198 more of size 6. The gain was 0.21%, and the figure above reproduces it.

At our tracker’s last snapshot that day, #469 was the highest open claim (it was still a draft) and #464 the highest non-draft claim. Neither was ever reviewed. Both, like their parent block, rest on an interface that another pull request (#451) disputes, as our closing note said. By 13 October the reviewed record had moved far past them, on a different line of work, so we closed both.

The tracker we built to follow the race is still here as the race tracker, and it is how this site began. Its claims feed stopped updating on 11 October at 17:41 UTC; it still reads the reviewed κ live.
