When the crowd gets a frontier model

On 7 October founding member Doug Colkitt opened a public repository around one question: how much faster than n log n can two large numbers provably be multiplied? Within days a crowd of people and their AI agents was moving the answer. Aska Digital’s essay of 13 October 2026, kept as published, asks why that worked.

The essay, as published

On 7 October someone opened a public repository holding one question: how much faster than n log n can you prove that two large numbers can be multiplied? Five days later it has 857 pull requests from 172 people, and the reviewed answer has moved 3.6×.

01//Why it moved

Why it moved

Two things were different. The first is obvious: a model will read a long technical argument with you and draft the next step. That gets you a candidate.

The second matters more. Every result that moves the number arrives with a script that checks it. Nobody has to trust the person who sent it. You run their verifier on your own machine and it passes or it fails. A wrong answer costs its author some time and costs everyone else nothing.

That is what makes a crowd usable on a problem like this. Without the check, 857 pull requests is just noise nobody can afford to read. With it, the work composes: pull requests name the exact constructions they build on, and those belong to other people.

02//Most of it is not the answer

Most of it is not the answer

Look at what the pull requests actually contain and the shape of a working research community shows up. Alongside the improvements there are independent re-verifications, speedups to other people's checking scripts, formal proofs in Lean, and a steady stream of honest negative results: this route is closed, here is the obstruction. Of 857 pull requests, 152 are merged. The rest are not waste. A closed route nobody has to try again is worth something, and the crowd is cheap enough to afford them.

03//What this is not

What this is not

The result is conditional. It inherits a framework from an earlier paper, and the repository says plainly that its finite checks are not a complete machine-checked theorem, and that none of this is a claim about how fast your computer multiplies numbers today. The honesty is part of why it works: the scope of every claim is written down, so the next person knows exactly what they are standing on.

04//Our part

Our part

We have been working in two of these.

Circuit constructions. A separate repository collects the smallest known circuits for arithmetic in binary fields, measured in AND and XOR gates. Smaller circuits mean cheaper encryption work, particularly in secure multi-party computation and zero-knowledge proofs. We contributed multipliers for four fields it did not cover. Two of them came in below the published baseline. All five were re-checked by the author against the pack's own verifier, and merged.

The exponent race. We held the top open claim for several hours on 11 October, at κ = 0.00135058, by re-timing an existing construction. The race has since moved a long way past it, on a different line of work. That is the honest state of it: a real contribution, briefly in front, now behind.

05//Why it matters

Why it matters

The interesting part is not the constant. It is that a machine-checkable problem statement turns a crowd with good tools into something that functions like a laboratory, in days, at almost no coordination cost. The number of problems that can be posed this way is much larger than the number that have been.

06//Corrections

Corrections, 14 and 15 October 2026

Foundation0 checked this essay against the sources before republishing it. Six lines need a note: one leaves out who opened the repository, two have slips, and three say more than the evidence does. The essay above is unchanged; here is what the record supports.

  1. On 7 October someone opened a public repository

    Named. The repository was opened by founding member Doug Colkitt (@Mister-Meeseeks), who maintains it and selects every reviewed record.

  2. Five days later it has 857 pull requests

    Six days. The repository opened on 7 October and the counts are from 13 October, 08:10 UTC: six days, as the essay’s own “6 days old” tile says.

  3. We have been working in two of these.

    Two of what. Two kinds of crowd-checkable problem: circuit constructions, and the exponent race.

  4. We held the top open claim for several hours on 11 October.

    Not supported by our own data. Our tracker saw PR #469 (κ = 0.001350580884553120) only between its appearance at 17:32 UTC and the tracker’s last snapshot at 17:41 UTC. In that window it was the top open claim, and it was still a draft; higher claims from other contributors (#480 and #505, for example) carry commits from later that day. It was never reviewed, and Aska closed it on 13 October. Read: briefly the top open claim on our tracker. The full account is on the project page.

  5. All five were re-checked by the author against the pack's own verifier, and merged.

    Four, not five, and the re-check is ours. The table lists four fields. The repository’s history shows the maintainer merged the pull request and credited it in the README; it does not show a re-run by the maintainer. Foundation0 re-ran the pack’s verifier on all four circuits on 13 October, and they pass. None of them is a record.

  6. Smaller circuits mean cheaper encryption work, particularly in secure multi-party computation and zero-knowledge proofs.

    Too broad. In those settings the cost is driven mostly by AND gates, while XOR gates are nearly free, and every gate these circuits save is an XOR; the AND counts are unchanged. Smaller XOR counts mean less hardware and fewer instructions in bit-sliced software.

Corrections compiled from the circuit pack’s history (PR #1), this site’s tracker feed (chart-data.json, generated 11 Oct 2026, 17:41 UTC) and the race’s pull requests #469 and #464.
