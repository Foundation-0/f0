Faint shadows in old starlight

A small planet crossing its star dims it by less than the flicker in a month of measurements. Founding member eumemic, working with an AI coding agent, combed NASA’s TESS data for four million stars. On 14 October 2026 the search counts 665 candidate signals that the official searches had passed over.

00//First principles

Computed from geometry

You cannot see the planet. You can see its shadow.

A planet around another star is a speck lost in a glare millions of times brighter. But if its orbit is edge-on to us, once per orbit it passes in front of the star and blocks a little of the light.

Definitions

Transit: a planet passing in front of its star, as seen from here. Light curve: a star’s brightness, measured again and again over time. ppm: parts per million; 1,000 ppm is a dip of 0.1%.

How much light does the planet block? The star dims by the share of its disc the planet covers: the ratio of the two discs’ areas, (planet radius ÷ star radius)². An Earth crossing the Sun would block about 84 ppm, one part in 11,900, for about 13 hours, once a year.

NASA’s TESS satellite (the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite) has been photographing most of the sky since 2018, one strip at a time, for about 27 days per strip. Each 27-day stretch is called a sector. For a small planet, the dip in one sector is often smaller than the random flicker of the measurements, so no single look shows it.

The trick is that a planet is a clock. Its dips come back at exactly the same interval, its period. Guess the period right, cut years of data into pieces that long and stack them, and the random flicker averages away while the dip adds up. Guess wrong by a few seconds and, hundreds of orbits later, the dips no longer line up.

So a search tries many thousands of periods for every star. That is the work this project did, for millions of stars, with an AI coding agent writing and running the code. The figures after this one use the project’s own light curves to show why it works, and how the search checks itself.

01//Find the heartbeat

Real TESS data · TIC 4206066

Stack seven years of starlight at the right rhythm, and a dip appears.

This is the real record of one nearby orange dwarf star, TIC 4206066: 4,075 brightness measurements, each averaged over 30 minutes, from three sectors in 2018, 2020 and 2025.

In September 2026 Pavel Rabtsevich reported two transit-like signals on this star. The project’s method follows Rabtsevich’s, and its search found both signals again without being told where they were: periods of 3.18278 and 11.13274 days, against Rabtsevich’s 3.182785 and 11.13274. The inner one would be a planet about 1.4 times Earth’s width, blocking 398 ppm for 1.7 hours.

Each measurement wobbles by about 300 ppm, so a 398 ppm dip lasting three or four measurements is invisible in the raw record. Choose a trial period and the figure cuts the record into pieces that long and lays them on top of each other.

How exact must the guess be? The record spans 2,577 days, about 810 orbits. Get the period wrong by 8 seconds and the error builds up to 810 × 8 s, nearly two hours, by 2025: the late dips slide right off the early ones. That is why a search has to test hundreds of thousands of periods per star.

The bottom panel is such a search, which Foundation0 ran on this record: 373,825 trial periods from 1 to 13 days. Two peaks stand clear of the noise, one for each signal. The shorter peaks are noise, and the echoes at half and twice the period.

02//Why stacking works

Real TESS data · 28 transits

Noise shrinks with the square root of the number of looks.

Average N noisy measurements and the noise in the average shrinks by √N: four times the data, half the noise. The dip does not shrink, so its signal-to-noise ratio grows as √N.

Signal-to-noise ratio

The dip’s depth divided by the typical error in measuring it. Below about 7 a dip is easily mimicked by chance; well above 10 it stands out.

The record of TIC 4206066 holds 28 transits of the inner signal: 6 in 2018, 8 in 2020 and 14 in 2025. Stack them one at a time, in the order they were observed. One transit is three or four measurements, each off by about 280 ppm, so a 400 ppm dip is lost (signal-to-noise about 3.5).

After the six transits of 2018 the ratio reaches about 7, the level NASA’s own pipeline needs in a single sector. With all 28 it is about 15. The measured points follow the √N curve closely; where they stray, it is real noise, not a smooth model.

The project found that this is exactly how its candidates were missed: more than half of them (271 of the 494 checked on 13 October) never reach 7.1 in any single sector. Only by joining every sector does the dip stand out.

Signal-to-noise here is Foundation0’s simple estimate (mean depth over the scatter of all points, divided by √n); the project quotes 15.6 for this signal with its own fit.

03//Turn the sky upside down

Measured on 798,769 stars

To count your false alarms, search data where the answer must be “nothing”.

Test hundreds of thousands of periods on millions of stars and noise will sometimes line up into something that looks like a dip. How often? The project measured it with a simple trick.

Turn a light curve upside down. Every real dip, from a planet or an eclipsing pair of stars, becomes a bump, and a search for dips ignores bumps. The random wiggles of noise go both ways, so the flipped curve still has “dips”. Anything the search finds in it must be a false alarm.

The project ran its full pipeline on the flipped light curves of 798,769 stars, in two independent samples of about 400,000, and scaled the count up to the 4,039,203 stars it searched. Step 2 shows one of the false alarms it found: it passed every one of the 17 automated tests of LEO-Vetter, a published vetting tool, and would look convincing in a catalogue.

Step 3 is the payoff. Among clean signals at signal-to-noise 9 or more, the project found 726 in the real data and expects 96 from noise alone. In the weaker band from 7.5 to 9 it found 382 and expects 314: most of those are noise. False alarms crowd at low strength, so tiers A, B and N require a strength (MES, below) of at least 10, and from the weaker band only the signals a separate classifier picks out are counted.

MES

The multiple event statistic: the signal-to-noise of all of a signal’s transits combined, as the search measures it.

04//What survived

Full-sky search · 8–14 Oct 2026

Seven and a half million repeating dips. Almost all of them are not planets.

Most repeating dips are noise, spacecraft glitches, or two stars eclipsing each other, often a faint pair sharing a camera pixel with the target. TESS pixels are 21 arcseconds wide, so one pixel can hold several stars.

Where the 665 come from

  • A several signals on one star20 + 24
  • B one signal, clean field297 + 34
  • N a neighbour within 15″, cleared statistically177
  • L1 weaker band, picked by a classifier113

Full-sky + earlier searches, on 14 October 2026 (candidates.json). Tiers A and B come from the clean signals after binary-star, grazing and literature checks. Tier N signals have a neighbour star close enough to fake the dip; a statistical test (TRICERATOPS, combined with where the light dropped) must still put the chance of a false positive below one half. Tier L1 comes from the noisy 7.5–9 band, picked by a classifier trained on flipped light curves. Not counted: tier C (grazing orbits, or a star whose Gaia measurements hint at a companion), likely binaries and likely false alarms.

Why the official searches passed them over

  • 82%are on stars seen only in wide-field images, which NASA’s multi-sector searches do not cover
  • 55%never reach 7.1, NASA’s single-sector threshold, in any one sector
  • 43%would already reach signal-to-noise 9 with data through September 2022; 87% with data through September 2025

Of the 494 full-sky candidates in tiers A, B and N (414 from the first pass, 80 from the re-search), as updated on 13 October (findability.md). The data were not new; the signals were weak in any one look.

05//Who did what

From the git history and the README

One researcher and an AI agent, one week, and outside fixes through pull requests.

The first commit was on 7 October; by 9 October the full-sky search counted 543 candidates, and on 14 October it counts 665. The project says it was “built with Claude Code (Anthropic)”.

Human

eumemic · founding member

  • Owns the project and its decisions: the method (after Pavel Rabtsevich’s study), the star samples, the checks, and the rules for what counts as a candidate.
  • Ran the search of 4,039,203 stars on rented cloud computers, and the earlier searches of nearby K and M dwarfs (stars smaller and cooler than the Sun), faint M dwarfs and the hosts of 6,430 TESS Objects of Interest (stars already known to have planets or planet candidates).
  • Measured the search’s own failure rates: flipped light curves, injected fake transits, known planets found again blind.
  • Reviewed and merged the outside fixes, acknowledged them in the README, and re-ran the affected steps.

Digital

Claude Code · Anthropic

  • That covers the pipeline code, the cloud search, the vetting, the validation notes and the explainer film (whose narration is a synthetic ElevenLabs voice, with music made in Suno).

What Aska Digital contributed

Ten pull requests, all merged by eumemic on 13 and 14 October 2026 (UTC): four fixes to the pipeline, an independent check, the first version of the website, a fix to the transit predictions, tests and documentation. 9 of the 12 files in the project’s tests/ folder came from them.

  1. PR #4Merged

    The false-alarm classifier was tested on stars it had seen in training.

    Its check let signals from the same star sit on both sides of the split between training and testing, which flatters the score. Grouped by star, 8 tier L1 candidates fell below the cut and 11 others joined, which gave the count of 533 on 13 October. The classifier has been retrained this way since.

  2. PR #6Merged

    Light curves with an empty error column were silently thrown away.

    In the earlier searches that removed 24–82% of the light curves from QLP (MIT’s Quick-Look Pipeline, one of the main sources of TESS light curves) in Sectors 94–97 and 101–104. With the fix, 1,770,906 full-sky stars gained light curves; eumemic searched them again the same day and counted 120 new candidates (6 in tier A, 51 B, 25 N, 38 L1). That re-search, with the classifier retrained on its signals, took the count from 533 to 671 the same day.

  3. PR #12Merged

    Re-delivered data files were not indexed.

    NASA’s archive re-issued Sectors 74–79 of the same product as version 2; the index looked only for version 1, so it held none of those light curves. They were included in the re-search.

  4. PR #13Merged

    Fast-moving stars were never matched to the Gaia catalogue.

    Matching without correcting for motion missed stars moving faster than about 0.19 arcseconds a year, so a binary-star check could never fire for them. The authors applied it: 3 counted candidates now have a Gaia RUWE above 1.4 (RUWE is Gaia’s measure of how much a star’s position wobbles; above 1.4 hints at a companion star) and moved to tier C.

  5. PR #7Merged

    An independent check of the signals the classifier fix moved.

    The 29 signals that changed sides under PR #4 were measured again, transit by transit, in 210 public light curves with a simple local baseline. Seven of the eight counted candidates among them keep their dips; one depends on how the data are cleaned. It changes no tiers and proves no planets, and it says so.

  6. PR #8Merged

    The project’s website.

    A summary of the search, a candidate explorer, a transit planner and the methods, now at eumemic.github.io/exoplanets. Aska built the first version; the project now computes every number on it from the repository’s own tables each time it is rebuilt, on every push.

Also merged on 14 October: #14 planned TESS sectors in the transit predictions, #15 a data dictionary for the published tables, #16 automated tests that check the tables agree, and #17 a guide to the repository. 16 of Aska’s 19 commits carry a Claude Opus 5.5 co-author line; the commits behind #4, #6 and #7 record no AI tool.

06//How sure

CandidatesNot confirmedNot peer reviewed

Candidates, checked many ways, and testable by anyone with a telescope.

None of the 665 is a confirmed planet. Confirming one needs other telescopes: ground-based photometry, sharp imaging to rule out a close neighbour, or measurements of the star’s wobble. The authors expect false positives of tens of percent in tier B.

The count will change

It has changed with almost every update: 543 on 9 October, 546 on 11 October, 528 on 12 October, then 530, 533, 671 and 664 on 13 October, and 665 on 14 October. Still open when last noted (13 October): a timing error in archive files for Sectors 99–104, whose corrected files had not yet been delivered. Several signals were also posted independently by other researchers within days.

The test that will settle it

Before the data exist, the project committed each candidate’s future transit times to the public repository: 712 registered candidates (665 still counted) and 45,781 predicted transits through June 2027; the median uncertainty of each candidate’s next transit is 11 minutes (14 October). A real planet must dim its star on schedule, by the predicted amount. Anyone can check.
