Witness Map: where quantum turns classical

Every object from an electron to the Milky Way is asked the same question: can it be in two places at once? The answer comes down to a race between two clocks, and one substance in the universe has nothing watching it at all.

Exploration Not peer reviewed No new result claimed Order-of-magnitude model · 16 Oct 2026

Explored by Aska Digital with Claude (Anthropic). Discussion and open questions: issue #10.

00//The question

First principles

A wave can only interfere with itself if no record of its path exists anywhere.

Electrons go through both slits; planets do not. The usual story blames the size of the planet. A sharper story asks who is watching.

In a two-slit experiment, an electron’s wave passes through both slits and the two halves overlap into stripes. Put a detector at the slits and the stripes vanish. It is tempting to say the detector knocks the electron off course. In 1998 Dürr, Nonn and Rempe tested exactly that: their detector’s push on the atoms was far too small to wash out the stripes, yet the stripes still vanished. What destroyed them was the record itself, the fact that the detector now held which-path information.

Any photon that bounces off an object and flies away is such a record. So is a gas atom that collides with it, and so is the object’s own heat glow. Physicists call the loss of wave behaviour this way decoherence. A planet is not quiet; it is one of the most watched objects there is.

Wave clock

How long the object’s wave needs to spread across the gap between the two places: m·Δx²/ℏ. Heavier things spread slower, so their clock runs long.

Witness clock

How long until something in its surroundings carries off a record of which place it is in: one over the rate of informative hits.

Their ratio is the witness number, W = wave clock ÷ witness clock. Below 1, the wave crosses the gap before anything can record which side the object is on, and it can act quantum. Above 1, the witnesses win and it acts classical.

02//What it shows

At a 250 nm gap

The lab-proven waves land on the wave side, and dark matter is never watched.

  1. The model agrees with experiment. All four objects that have been seen acting as waves (an electron, a rubidium atom, a buckyball and a 2,000-atom molecule) land left of the line, at W from about 10−13 to 0.05. Raise the gap and the giant molecule crosses first, watched by its own heat glow.
  2. Everything bigger is flooded with witnesses at home. The closest call is an interstellar dust grain at W ≈ 107. A virus in air scores about 1016, a cat about 1048, Earth about 1081.
  3. Mass counts through one door, the wave clock. Heavy things spread so slowly that a trickle of witnesses settles them. Silence the witnesses and a virus can cross: in a 4 K vacuum it scores W ≈ 0.04, which is why a virus in two places at once inside a cold vacuum has been proposed as an experiment.
  4. Dark matter has no witnesses at all. No light bounces off it and it does not collide with gas, so it stays on the wave side at every gap, however heavy the particle. That lack of witnesses is part of what makes a galaxy-sized dark matter wave possible: a particle of 10−22 eV has a wavelength of about a kiloparsec.
  5. A black hole watches, but feebly. Even ten horizon radii from Sagittarius A*, a wave dark matter particle split by 1 µm would wait about 10170 years for the horizon to record it. The horizon only beats ordinary witnesses for objects that are already classical.

03//How it is computed

physics.js

Count the hits, weigh what each one learns, compare with the wave.

wave clock = m·Δx²/ℏ  ·  witness rate Γ = Σ (hits per second) × (information per hit)  ·  W = Γ × wave clock

  • Hits per second. Photons from the surroundings (a full thermal spectrum for each source), gas atoms, the solar wind and the object’s own heat glow. Cross-sections are geometric for objects bigger than the wavelength and fall off as (size/wavelength)4 for smaller ones; the electron uses the standard electron–photon value. Molecules glow 100 times more weakly than solid grains.
  • Information per hit. A hit with wavelength λ can tell the two places apart only if the gap is comparable to λ. The fraction is 1 − exp(−(2πΔx/λ)²/3): tiny for gaps much smaller than λ, one full record for larger gaps.
  • Black hole horizon. Γ = G6M5m2Δx4/(ℏc10b10) at distance b, plus an electric version for the electron. Valid only when the gap is much smaller than the distance.

Checks against published numbers

  • Light and gas scattering on a 10 µm grain match the Joos–Zeh decoherence table to within a factor of about 3 for the Big Bang afterglow, sunlight and air.
  • The horizon formula reproduces both worked examples in the Wald paper: Earth split by its own radius beside a Sun-mass black hole (paper ~10 µs, here ~20 µs) and an electron split by 1 m (paper ~1043 years, here ~4×1042 years).
  • A buckyball here loses its wave behaviour when heated to about 2,500–3,000 K, in line with the 2004 Vienna heating experiment.

What it leaves out

Objects sit still: fast motion, internal structure and spin are ignored. W is per particle, while wave dark matter’s galaxy-wide wave is a crowd effect of enormous numbers of particles sharing one wave, which this map does not model. Gravity’s only witness here is a black hole horizon; nudges from passing stars are treated as zero. Every number is good to a factor of a few, on a scale that spans more than 150 powers of ten.

Corrections, kept on the record

Three claims made during the exploration did not survive it. The detector’s push does not explain lost interference; the record does. “The line is set by witnesses, not size” was too strong; mass enters through the wave clock. And a quoted figure that the Big Bang afterglow pins a dust grain “in about a nanosecond” became about a millisecond for a 10 µm grain split by 1 mm.

04//Open questions

Issue #10

If a black hole shapes dark matter, it is by feeding it, not by watching it.

The map rules out the strong version of the idea that a galaxy’s black hole “collapses” its surroundings into particles. It leaves a more interesting version open.

A spinning black hole can amplify very light particles into a cloud around itself, a process called superradiance. The cloud settles into the same energy levels as a hydrogen atom, with gravity in place of electric attraction: a “gravitational atom”. Here the old intuition that atoms are little solar systems becomes literally true, because the same equation, a wave held by a pull that weakens with distance, has the same solution at any scale.

  • Do lensing anomalies attributed to wave dark matter (Amruth et al., 2023) correlate with the lens galaxy’s central black hole mass or spin?
  • Model the crowd wave (one shared field under its own gravity) instead of single particles.
  • Put a number on gravity-only witnesses other than horizons.
  • Draw hydrogen and a gravitational atom side by side, at the same scale.
