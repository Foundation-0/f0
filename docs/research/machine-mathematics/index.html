Research // machine-written mathematics

After the 722

On 6 October 2026 OpenAI published 722 mathematics manuscripts written by one of its AI models, grouped into 372 families of results. No human referee had checked them. Within a week, people working with their own AI agents had tightened the numbers in several. Here is the whole collection, the seven families our founding members built on, and how much room the first proofs left.

The repository says the manuscripts and their proof files were “produced by an internal OpenAI model”, and it is plain about the risk: “Some of the unformalized results could have issues.” The first commit is signed “Anonymous” and dated 6 October 2026, 14:58 Pacific time. The folder dates show how fast the writing went: 717 of the 722 manuscripts are dated between 22 September and 5 October.

What a Lean scope document is

Lean is a proof checker: a program that accepts a proof only if every step follows from the rules, taking nothing on trust. For 235 of the 372 families, OpenAI linked a scope document, a plan saying which statements in that family are to be checked in Lean. A plan is not a finished check. And a Lean proof confirms only the statement it was given; whether that statement says what the paper says is a separate question. OpenAI’s own catalogue marks its formal proofs as made by agents and not yet reviewed.

The next day, 7 October, OpenAI withdrew three manuscripts because “a sign error invalidates a stabilization-trace cancellation argument”, revised fourteen others and added formal proofs. By its own count, 300 of 719 top-line results, about 42%, were then formalized.

Plain words

Preprint: a paper made public before any referee has checked it. Every manuscript in the collection is a preprint, and so is almost everything on this page.

Plain words

Bound: a proved limit, such as “this takes at most so many steps”. A constant is a fixed number inside a bound. Making the constant better makes the whole statement stronger.

Our earlier reading

Aska’s essay Math, faster (7 Oct) read the collection on day one and argued that checking proofs, not finding them, is now the slow part. It predates the withdrawals above.

Seven families, built on within a week.

Founding members Doug Colkitt, rohanarun, eumemic and Ryan Shea took seven of the 372 families further. Swapnil Jain, with a check from Aska, carried one of them (family 130) into arithmetic on bits. Others joined every effort and are named below where the record names them.

Entry · family 109Algorithms and complexity

Integer multiplication below n log n

1 manuscript, dated 23 Sep 2026 · no Lean scope document

Since 2019, two n-digit numbers can be multiplied in about n·log n steps; back in 1971 Schönhage and Strassen had guessed that nothing could do better. OpenAI’s manuscript claims a sliver less on the simplest model of a computer, a multitape Turing machine: n·(log n)1−κ steps with κ = 2−182. A bigger κ is a bigger saving.

Entry · family 130Algorithms and complexity

Exact Fourier transforms below n log n

2 manuscripts, dated 25 Sep 2026 · Lean scope document (for the finite circuit result only)

The discrete Fourier transform splits a list of numbers into its frequencies, the way an ear splits a chord into notes; the fast Fourier transform does it in about n·log n steps. OpenAI’s manuscript claims an exact method, with no rounding, in n·(log n)1−δ steps with δ = 10−13.

Before → afterδ: 10−13 → 0.0012331685checked in Lean on 12 Oct, about 12 billion times larger
  • Ryan Shea, founding member, with Jeremy Kothe, whose circuit it uses: δ = 0.0012331685, rebuilt from source and checked in Lean by the Big O Speedrun leaderboard, which ranks it first and credits both. By their own account, Ryan Shea’s part was converting the circuit and checking this instance of it; the Lean engine is Jacob Sussman’s, and one extension came from Nil Mamano.
  • eumemic, founding member: claimed δ = 607/1,250,000 ≈ 0.00049 on 9 Oct, with an exact certificate but no formal proof. It was the best claim for about six hours.
  • Swapnil Jain: mobius-x1 carries the same construction into arithmetic on bits, for the Möbius transform over GF(2). Aska wrote and ran the full-network check behind its Theorem 2 (merged PR #1).

Entry · family 160Combinatorics

Superexponential van der Waerden numbers

1 manuscript, dated 23 Sep 2026 · Lean scope document

Colour the numbers 1, 2, 3, … with r colours. If the row is long enough, one colour must hold k evenly spaced numbers, like 4, 9, 14, 19. The van der Waerden number is the shortest row that forces this. OpenAI’s manuscript claims to prove it is longer than kc·k·⌊log2 r⌋ with c = 1/100000.

Before → afterc: 1/100000 → 1/9about 11,111 times larger, conditional on the manuscript
  • rohanarun, founding member, with Codex and Claude, started the repository on 9 Oct: c = 1/200, then 1/193, then 1/25, and a proof that this line of attack cannot pass 1/24.
  • artuskg, with GPT-6 Pro and Codex: 1/19, from a new construction.
  • DaysSky, with Claude: 1/16, then 2/25, then 1/9.
  • A partial Lean check of 84 statements, by rohanarun, is open for review (PR #10).

Entry · family 161Combinatorics

Counterexamples to Sidorenko’s conjecture and the forcing conjecture

1 manuscript, dated 23 Sep 2026 · Lean scope document

A graph is a network of dots and lines. Sidorenko’s conjecture predicted that every two-sided pattern (a bipartite graph) appears in any network at least as often as in a random network with the same number of lines. OpenAI’s manuscript claims a counterexample: a pattern on 35 dots.

Before → afterone counting constant: 1641 → 189and no smaller certificate than 35 dots inside the manuscript’s own template
  • rohanarun, founding member, with Codex and Claude: enumerated every candidate shape up to 12 points, killed each with an exact certificate, and found nothing smaller inside the template. Then cut the constant in one counting step from 1641 to 1032, 978 and finally 189.
  • The minimum holds only inside that template, not for every possible counterexample.

Entry · family 111Algorithms and complexity

One-sample matroid prophet inequalities against an almighty adversary

1 manuscript, dated 23 Sep 2026 · Lean scope document

Items arrive one at a time, in an order chosen by an opponent who knows everything. You keep or drop each on the spot, may keep only sets that a fixed rule allows (a matroid, such as “at most k items”), and know just one earlier sample of each item’s value. OpenAI’s manuscript claims to prove that a strategy always earns a fixed fraction of the best possible total.

Before → afterfraction: 2−293 → more than (35/32)·2−31 ≈ 5.1×10−102−293 is what the manuscript’s own derivation gives; its headline says 2−310
  • Ryan Shea, founding member, with Codex: six versions in two days, from 2−36 on 10 Oct to (35/32)·2−31 on 11 Oct, each with exact certificates.

03//The slack

Six refinements · 52 public steps

First proofs leave room. People found it.

A first proof only has to work. To keep it short, its author picks safe, loose numbers wherever the argument allows, much as a first route to a new place takes the roads you already know. That looseness is slack. Tightening it rarely needs a new idea at first, just patience, and people with AI agents have patience to spare.

The numbers here span a huge range, from 2−293, a decimal point followed by 88 zeros, up to 1641. So the chart uses a log scale: each step along the axis multiplies the value by ten, so the length of a line counts how many times over the value changed.

Four of the six numbers are better when bigger: a bigger saving, a bigger guaranteed fraction. Two are better when smaller: a smaller constant, a smaller grid. Their lines run leftward, from the hollow circle to the filled one.

Read with care

A line’s length shows how loose the first number was, not how important the result is. The 1079-fold jump in the matroid fraction is still a fraction below one in a billion, and nobody’s computer multiplies faster because of the integer-multiplication race.

04//What “conditional” means

Read before you cite

Most of this stands on a foundation nobody has checked.

A conditional result says: if that earlier result is true, then this one is too. Here the earlier result is a machine-written preprint. A careful refinement of a flawed proof is still a refinement of a flawed proof.

Every number in section 03 began in an OpenAI manuscript that no human referee has checked. The people who improved them say so in their own repositories: “conditional”, “not peer reviewed”, “no human review”. What they checked, they checked exactly: certificates in exact arithmetic, every case of a finite search, every input of a circuit. Those checks confirm their own step. They cannot confirm the manuscript underneath.

A proof checked end to end in Lean is different. The Fourier record was rebuilt from source by an outside leaderboard and accepted by Lean’s kernel against that leaderboard’s exact statement of the problem, so it does not lean on OpenAI’s prose at all. What remains open is whether that formal statement says exactly what we mean in words, which is why human review is still listed as pending.

This is not a hypothetical worry. On 7 October, one day after release, OpenAI withdrew three manuscripts in family 032: a sign error broke one argument, and two papers that depended on it went too. The family shrank from eight manuscripts to five.

“For centuries, finding a proof was the hard part and checking it was routine. That ratio inverted on October 6.”

From Aska’s essay Math, faster, 7 Oct 2026.
