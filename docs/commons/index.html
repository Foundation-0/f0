Commons · free tools for the public good

Free tools that show their working

The commons is where Foundation0 builds free, public-benefit apps, meant in time for people and small businesses who cannot pay for software, in fields such as medicine, nutrition and personal finance. One exists today: Nutrak. This page explains what it does, from first principles, and exactly how far it has come.

00//Why a commons

Aska Digital essays · not peer reviewed

The money skips the public good.

Software costs almost nothing to copy, so a useful tool could reach everyone. In practice it reaches whoever someone can bill.

In The Money Skips the Public Good (19 August 2026), Aska Digital read Canada’s “AI for All” strategy line by line. Its equity, its loans and its adoption subsidies all pay for AI that can bill for itself: “Every pipeline the strategy built requires the thing it funds to be able to price its own value.” What falls through is “the AI that helps a community without a payer, the literacy that has no invoice, the inclusion nobody can bill for.” The essay’s remedy is about structure, not slogans: “make the good legally un-ownable, and then it can be funded without being bought.”

A week later, The Institution Is the Bottleneck (27 August 2026) asked why health care, schools and governments keep failing to adopt tools that work. Its answer: “a technology inherits the incentive structure of the institution it lands in, and it will not outrun it.” It ends with a test worth putting to any free tool, including ours: “who is accountable for the outcome, in what unit, and what happens to them if it does not improve?”

The commons is where Foundation0 tries to act on both essays. We are at the start. The app below is real, free and honest about its limits, but it has not yet been built with, or tested by, the people the commons is for. We say so in every section.

What we hold a commons tool to

  1. 01Free, with no account

    Nothing to pay and nothing to sign up for; the tool is the whole product.

  2. 02Your data stays with you

    The work happens on your device. Nothing personal is sent to a server.

  3. 03Small enough for a weak connection

    A slow or expensive connection should not decide who can use it.

  4. 04Shows its working

    Every number has a receipt: where it came from and what kind of number it is.

  5. 05Says what it cannot do

    Unbuilt features say so. A guess is labelled a guess.

  6. 06Has an owner and a measure

    Someone named, accountable for a stated result, in a stated unit.

01//Nutrak · Aska Digital

Live demoNot clinically validatedTarget engine not public

Nutrak: a tracker that shows its work.

Most trackers give one confident number and turn red the moment a day falls short. Nutrak puts a plain-words receipt under every target, and judges vitamins and minerals on a week of eating, never on a single day.

“How much calcium do I need?” sounds like it has one answer. It does not: needs differ from person to person, and what you ate is uncertain too. Nutrak’s code calls its answer a “trust contract”, with five rules:

  1. Every number carries a receipt. A “why this number?” button explains, in words, which official table a target came from and what kind of number it is, with 26 linked glossary terms.
  2. Energy is a range, never a point: 2,645–3,045 kcal a day for the demo person.
  3. Judge the week. Vitamins and minerals are judged on a 7-day (or 30-day) average. See why below.
  4. Missing is not low. A nutrient you have not logged shows “no data”, never 0% or “under”.
  5. A guess is a proposal. A photo estimate appears as an amber “estimate — edit to confirm” and never enters your log until you approve it.

What is real today

Live
Free, installable, works offline after the first visit; your log stays in your browser. Front end MIT-licensed.
Targets
A captured snapshot for one example person (a 35-year-old man), computed on 30 Aug 2026 by an engine that is not public yet. The numbers you type do not change them, and the app says so.
Previews
Photo, barcode and free-text logging. The photo flow uses a fixed example and recognizes nothing.
Checks
81 trust-contract checks and 42 install-and-offline checks in the repository, all passing when re-run for this page. The public history records a reviewer’s blocking finding: the test command had been skipping the second set.
Not yet
Clinical validation, a user study, peer review. It is not medical advice.
For the commons
Free, offline and small, which suits a weak connection. But English only, energy only in kcal, and no food database, so you must know what your food contains.

02//Explainer · noise and signal

Real demo data · 24–30 Aug 2026

One day is noise. A week is a signal.

Your body stores calcium, so one low day says almost nothing about you. A tracker that judges every day raises five alarms in an ordinary week; one that judges the week gives one calm, honest signal.

These are the demo person’s logged calcium values for one week, from Nutrak’s public demo data. The light uses Nutrak’s own thresholds against the 1,000 mg target: below 75% is “under”, below 100% is “approaching”, and 100% or more is “good”.

An honest footnote

An average is calmer, not immune. Nutrak’s glossary says one 5,000 mg calcium meal “can’t flip a pill”; with a plain 7-day mean it can, from 777 mg (approaching) to 1,384 mg (good). A longer window, such as the app’s 30-day option, dilutes one extreme day further.

03//Explainer · what a target means

Model · textbook rule

Enough for whom?

“You need 1,000 mg of calcium a day” sounds like a fact about you. It is a fact about a crowd.

Nobody can measure one person’s requirement directly, so scientists measure a group and describe the spread. The Estimated Average Requirement (EAR) is the intake that meets the needs of half the people of an age and sex: 800 mg of calcium for adults aged 19 to 50. The Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) sits about two standard deviations higher (a standard deviation is the typical distance of a person from the average), so it covers nearly everyone, about 97.5%: 1,000 mg. The RDA is a safety margin for a population, not your personal need. That is why Nutrak also divides your average by the EAR (its “adequacy ratio”), not only by the RDA.

04//The bar ahead

What would change our labels

The bar we have not reached yet.

A commons tool earns trust the way any instrument does: by being measured by someone other than its maker.
